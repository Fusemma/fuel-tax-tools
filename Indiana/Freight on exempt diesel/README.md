# Indiana freight on exempt diesel

Indiana sales tax on freight billed with exempt diesel, as of Nov. 9, 2026.

Diesel is exempt from Indiana sales tax, except heating oil [Ind. Code § 6-2.5-5-51(c)]. DOR's bulletin says that if the item sold is not subject to sales tax, the delivery charges for it are not either [Sales Tax Information Bulletin #92]. A few DOR rulings and audit letters taxed freight on exempt items. None of them is about fuel, and no Indiana court has ruled on it.

## Diesel and heating oil

Diesel has been exempt since July 1, 2017. Before that it was taxed unless the buyer or the use was exempt. Since Jan. 1, 2004 the law counts delivery charges in the taxed price, so freight on taxed diesel was taxed too [Ind. Code § 6-2.5-1-5(a)(4); Commissioner's Directive #23, July 2013]. DOR says the exemption "applies regardless of the purchaser or the use of the fuel" [Sales Tax Information Bulletin #15].

Heating oil has been taxed again since March 21, 2018 [Departmental Notice #12, June 2020]. Heating oil means "a special fuel that is burned in a boiler, furnace, or stove for heating or industrial processing purposes" [Ind. Code § 6-6-2.5-12]. Its delivery is taxed with it [Ind. Code § 6-2.5-1-5(a)(4)].

## Where DOR says it isn't taxed

Sales Tax Information Bulletin #92 is DOR's bulletin on delivery charges: "If the item being sold is not subject to sales tax, the delivery charges related to that item also are not subject to sales tax." DOR first said it in 2004 [Commissioner's Directive #23, Apr. 2004].

DOR's rental bulletin has a fuel example. A rental company refuels rented equipment on site, and the charges to deliver the fuel "are subject to sales tax to the extent the fuel is subject to sales tax, such as propane" [Sales Tax Information Bulletin #42, Example #3].

DOR has allowed a refund on it. A hospital's refund included the tax it paid on shipping "on invoices that included exempt items" [Final Order Denying Refund 04-20231820]. A 2013 letter says an item's exemption covers its whole price, "including delivery charges" [Letter of Findings 04-20120287].

## Where DOR taxed it

The CO2 rulings are the closest to us. The seller "delivers liquid CO2 to customers that is stored in a stainless steel cryogenic tank installed at the customer's premises" and charges its customers delivery fees. The CO2 was exempt for customers who use it in manufacturing, but "Delivery fees are included explicitly in gross retail income pursuant to IC 6-2.5-1-5(a)(4) and are, therefore, subject to sales tax" [Revenue Ruling #2016-05ST]. DOR repealed the 2009 ruling and replaced it with the 2016 one, which keeps the same delivery fee sentence [Revenue Ruling #2016-04ST; Revenue Ruling #2009-10 ST].

Letter of Findings 09-0457 is the only one of these that gives a reason. A steel processor's banding was exempt and its freight was taxed: "The reason the steel banding is exempt is that there is a specific exemption for that banding. The freight charge is a separate charge. Therefore, the freight charge is taxable" [Letter of Findings 09-0457]. The rule it cites, 45 IAC 2.2-4-3, was repealed in 2026 [LSA Document #24-395].

Two 2023 audit letters did the same with no reason given. One says "the shipping charges are not tax exempt" on exempt manufacturing equipment [Letter of Findings 04-20221058]. The other found three packaging purchases exempt, one at the "packaging purchase price less an $8.95 fuel surcharge" [Letter of Findings 04-20221047].

DOR's line that a seller collects tax "on the total amount of a transaction, including freight charges, unless that customer provides an exemption certificate" is from a 2011 letter, where the sales themselves were taxable [Letter of Findings 04-20100716].

## Refunds

The refund belongs to the customer: "Sales Tax is a tax borne by the customer (buyer) and can only be refunded to the customer (buyer), not to the retail merchant" [DOR Audit Manual]. The seller can't get a refund "unless the retail merchant refunds those taxes to the person from whom they were collected" [Ind. Code § 6-2.5-6-14.1].

A claim is due within three years after the later of the return's due date or the payment [Ind. Code § 6-8.1-9-1(a)]. For a tax filed four or more times a year, like sales tax, the due date counts as 31 days after the end of the calendar year [Ind. Code § 6-8.1-9-1(a); § 6-8.1-1-4.5]. That makes 2023's deadline Jan. 31, 2027. 2022 and earlier are closed.

## Open

DOR has written it both ways, and no DOR document squares the two.

Whether the CO2 rulings carry over to diesel, since diesel's exemption doesn't turn on the buyer's use.
